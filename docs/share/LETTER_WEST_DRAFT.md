Dear Dr. West,

*Scale* asks a question sharp enough to act on: do aggregated units obey
discoverable scaling laws, and what does the economics of the unit determine?
I took the question seriously enough to measure it — in a domain your book
does not cover — and I write to report the measurements and to offer a
conjecture back. Every prior below went on the record before its run
executed, and the refuted ones stay in the tables beside the held ones.

The units in question maintain knowledge rather than metabolize energy. Each
one holds a bounded, revisable model of a document corpus, tests what
arrives, and forgets what falls from use; I compare one large unit holding
everything against a federation of small units with a coordinator. The
five experiments and a rider, in one paragraph:

At matched size and equal durability of what they retain, the federation ran
about 5.2 times cheaper than the monolith — and retained more, because a
single attention budget stretched over everything forgets most of it. Across
an 8× size sweep the monolith's cost grew with exponent 1.28 against the
federation's 1.02, and a 25-fold cost spread at the largest size traced to
coordinator scan discipline alone — while the cost of the terminal unit
stayed invariant to a tenth of a percent, which I read as your invariant
terminal unit, observed in a new substrate. The optimum turned out interior
(three buckets, neither monolith nor finest split), and when the system
re-partitioned itself under its own economics, every walk converged to that
same granularity while landing in different partitions. The landscape
fragments: thirty-six structured starts found twenty-one local optima, with
one asymmetric family holding three quarters of the attractor mass within
1.4 percent of the cost floor — and the perfectly balanced partition sat
stranded in a measurably dear basin that single-folder perturbations escaped
only one time in three. Balance strands; asymmetry funnels.

A caution before you raise it: these exponents characterize one synthetic
landscape a modeler imposed, within one level of organization, and nothing
here points an arrow of social development. Both arrangements remain
super-linear — the federation avoids a diseconomy; it buys no economy of
scale. A real-corpus corroboration remains ahead, and the logs say so.

The conjecture I offer back, strictly as a conjecture: energy arrives scalar
at the supply layer, but the allocation layer of these units — what the unit
spends its budget *on* — behaves as a vector (track record, durability,
compression, use), and the allocation reshapes the landscape as it runs. If
that holds, the right object may not be a scalar exponent but a scaling
manifold, with the observed exponent as its frozen-landscape shadow. We
state the measurement we think would decide it and would gladly see you
replace it with a better one.

Three questions, then:

1. Does the mapping of your framework onto knowledge-maintenance economics
   treat the framework fairly — or can you name the category error?
2. What would you demand of a measurement before taking the vector-optimand
   conjecture seriously? Would you refute it as stated?
3. Of the internal-validity risks we log ourselves — synthetic corpus, one
   landscape, one decay model, modeled concurrency — which would you attack
   first?

A self-contained two-page methods note stands behind this letter, written so
that you need none of the project's vocabulary: what the unit does, what the
cost meter counts, how durability was equalized, and every prior with its
verdict. The full logs sit behind it, deterministic and replayable to the
byte.

With respect,

Michael Hauan
